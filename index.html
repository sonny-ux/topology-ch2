Sets and relations (Ch. 1)
concept
$x\sim y$
Equivalence relation
▸
Definition

A relation $\sim$ on $X$ that is reflexive ($x\sim x$), symmetric ($x\sim y\Rightarrow y\sim x$) and transitive ($x\sim y,\ y\sim z\Rightarrow x\sim z$).

Intuition

A rule for which points count as "the same".

Example

On a metric space with $A\subseteq X$: $x\sim_A y$ iff $x=y$ or both $x,y\in A$.

Non-example

$x\lt y$ on $\mathbb R$ is neither reflexive nor symmetric.

Often confused with

A partition: each one determines the other.

Where it appears

Quotient topology; HW7 Q1.

concept
$[x]=\{y:y\sim x\}$
Equivalence class
▸
Definition

The set of everything related to $x$.

Intuition

Everything that counts as the same as $x$.

Example

For $\sim_A$: $[a]=A$ for $a\in A$; $[x]=\{x\}$ for $x\notin A$.

Often confused with

$[x]$ is a set of points of $X$ but a single point of the quotient.

Where it appears

Quotients.

concept
$X=\textstyle\bigsqcup_\alpha P_\alpha$
Partition
▸
Definition

A collection of nonempty, pairwise disjoint subsets of $X$ whose union is $X$.

Intuition

Cut $X$ into pieces, no overlaps, nothing left out.

Example

The classes of any equivalence relation.

Non-example

$\{[0,1],[1,2]\}$ on $[0,2]$: the pieces overlap at $1$.

Where it appears

Munkres builds quotient spaces from partitions (§22).

Topologies and bases
concept
$U\in\mathcal T$
Open set
▸
Definition

A member of the topology $\mathcal T$.

Intuition

What "open" means depends entirely on the chosen topology; there is no open set without a topology.

Example

$(0,1)$ in $\mathbb R$; $[0,1)$ in $\mathbb R_\ell$.

Non-example

$[0,1)$ in standard $\mathbb R$.

Often confused with

"Not closed". Sets can be open, closed, both, or neither.

Where it appears

Every definition in the course.

concept
$x\in B\subseteq U$
Basis
▸
Definition

A collection $\mathcal B$ with (1) every point in some $B$, and (2) if $x\in B_1\cap B_2$ then some $B_3$ has $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$.

Intuition

Building blocks: you only need to understand small open sets around each point.

Example

Open intervals; open balls; $[a,b)$ for $\mathbb R_\ell$.

Non-example

Closed intervals in $\mathbb R$: $[0,1]\cap[1,2]=\{1\}$.

Often confused with

A topology. A basis generates one; it is not itself closed under unions.

Where it appears

Basis card, Method: show a set is open.

concept
$\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal T'$
Finer and coarser
▸
Definition

$\mathcal T'$ is finer than $\mathcal T$ if every $\mathcal T$-open set is $\mathcal T'$-open.

Intuition

Finer means more open sets, so it can tell more points apart and fewer sequences converge.

Example

$\mathbb R_\ell$ is finer than $\mathbb R$; discrete is finest, indiscrete coarsest.

Often confused with

Size of the open sets: a finer topology has more open sets, not bigger ones.

Where it appears

Lemma 13.3.

Closed sets and separation
concept
$X\setminus A\in\mathcal T$
Closed set
▸
Definition

A set whose complement is open.

Intuition

It contains all the points its own points crowd toward.

Example

$[a,b]$; single points in a Hausdorff space.

Non-example

$[0,1)$ and $\mathbb Q$ in $\mathbb R$.

Often confused with

"Not open".

Where it appears

HW7 Q1: closedness gives every outside point a ball missing $A$.

concept
$x\in A'$
Limit point
▸
Definition

$x$ is a limit point of $A$ if every neighbourhood of $x$ contains a point of $A$ other than $x$.

Intuition

Points of $A$ crowd in on $x$; $x$ itself may or may not be in $A$.

Example

$0$ for $(0,1)$ and for $\{1/n\}$.

Non-example

An isolated point of $A$, like $0$ in $\{0\}\cup[1,2]$.

Often confused with

A point of the closure: closure points include isolated points; limit points need not.

Where it appears

Theorem 17.6; why A must be closed in HW7.

concept
$U\cap V=\varnothing$
Hausdorff
▸
Definition

Any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods.

Intuition

Points can be put in separate bubbles, so limits are unique.

Example

Metric spaces; subspaces and products of Hausdorff spaces; $X/{\sim_A}$ with $A$ closed.

Non-example

The finite-complement topology on $\mathbb R$; $\mathbb R/(0,1)$; the line with two origins.

Often confused with

T₁ (points are closed): weaker. The finite-complement topology is T₁ but not Hausdorff.

Where it appears

Hausdorff card, Method: show a space is Hausdorff.

Continuous
▸
Definition

Preimages of open sets are open.

Intuition

Nearby points of $X$ go to nearby points of $Y$, phrased with open sets instead of distances.

Example

Any map out of a discrete space.

Non-example

The identity $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R_\ell$.

Often confused with

Open map (images of open sets open): a different, independent property.

Continuity card, Method.

concept
$X\to X^*$
Quotient (gluing)
▸
Definition

Given a partition $X^*$ of $X$ and $p:X\to X^*$ sending each point to its piece, give $X^*$ the topology in which $U$ is open iff $p^{-1}(U)$ is open.

Intuition

Glue points together. A set of glued points is open exactly when everything lying over it is open upstairs.

Example

$[0,1]$ with ends glued is a circle; crush a closed set to a point.

Non-example

Crushing a non-closed set can destroy Hausdorff: $\mathbb R/(0,1)$.

Often confused with

The subspace topology: that restricts to a subset; the quotient glues.

Where it appears

Quotient card, Method.

concept
$p^{-1}(p(C))=C$
Saturated set
▸
Definition

A set that is a union of whole pieces of the partition.

Intuition

It never contains part of a glued piece without the rest.

Example

For $\sim_A$: any set containing all of $A$ or none of it.

Non-example

A small ball around a point of $A$, when $A$ is bigger than the ball.

Where it appears

The engine of HW7 Q1(b): open saturated sets push down to open sets.

A topology is a choice of which sets are open. Almost everything here is a way to describe that choice economically (a basis) or to build new spaces from old ones (subspaces, products).
Methods · how problems here are solved
method
Check that a collection is a topology
“Show that 𝒯 is a topology on X”
▸
Recognize it

You are handed a collection of subsets and asked whether it satisfies the axioms.

Steps
  1. Check $\varnothing$ and $X$ are in it.

    Why: axiom (1). Easy to forget when the collection is described by a rule.

  2. Unions: take an arbitrary family $\{U_\alpha\}$ of members and show $\bigcup U_\alpha$ is a member.

    Why arbitrary: axiom (2) is for any number of sets, even uncountably many. Checking two at a time is not enough.

  3. Finite intersections: show $U_1\cap U_2$ is a member.

    Why two suffice: induction then gives any finite number.

    Why not infinite: $\bigcap_n(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)=\{0\}$ is not open in $\mathbb R$.

Uses

Topology.

Common mistakes
  • Checking only pairwise unions.
  • Forgetting the empty set in a rule such as "complements are finite".
Go deeper

For the finite-complement topology the axioms reduce to De Morgan: $X\setminus\bigcup U_\alpha=\bigcap(X\setminus U_\alpha)$ is finite, and $X\setminus(U_1\cap U_2)=(X\setminus U_1)\cup(X\setminus U_2)$ is finite.

method
Show a set is open
“Show U is open”, in a space given by a basis or a metric
▸
Recognize it

The topology comes from a basis (intervals, balls, boxes) and you must show a particular set is open.

Steps
  1. Take an arbitrary point $x\in U$.

    Why: openness is a condition at every point.

  2. Produce a basis element $B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$. In a metric space this is a ball $B(x,r)$; say how $r$ depends on $x$.

    Why: this is the definition of the topology generated by the basis.

  3. Prove $B\subseteq U$: take $y\in B$ and show $y\in U$.

    How: usually the triangle inequality: $d(z,y)\le d(z,x)+d(x,y)$.

Uses

Basis · Metric.

Common mistakes
  • Choosing one radius for all points: $r$ may depend on $x$.
  • Showing $x\in B$ but not $B\subseteq U$.
Go deeper

Other routes: write $U$ as a union of open sets, or as $f^{-1}(V)$ for a continuous $f$ and open $V$.

method
Compare two topologies
“Show 𝒯′ is finer than 𝒯”, “are these topologies the same?”
▸
Recognize it

Two topologies on the same set, each given by a basis.

Steps
  1. Write down bases for both.

    Why: Lemma 13.3 only needs bases.

  2. For each $x$ and each $B\ni x$ from the coarser candidate, find $B'$ from the finer one with $x\in B'\subseteq B$.

    Why: Lemma 13.3.

  3. For "strictly finer": exhibit one open set of the finer topology that is not open in the other.

    Example: $[0,1)$ is open in $\mathbb R_\ell$ but not in $\mathbb R$.

Uses

Lemma 13.3.

Common mistakes
  • Getting the direction backwards: the finer topology supplies the smaller sets.
§12 · Topological spaces
§12
$\varnothing,X\in\mathcal T,\ \ \bigcup U_\alpha\in\mathcal T,\ \ U_1\cap\dots\cap U_n\in\mathcal T$
Topology — the three axioms
▸
Symbols
  • $X$the underlying set
  • $\mathcal T$the topology: the collection of open sets
  • $U_\alpha$any family of members, of any size
  • $U_1,\dots,U_n$finitely many members
Origin

These are the properties of open sets in $\mathbb R$ that the rest of analysis actually uses. Taking them as the definition lets the same arguments run in spaces with no distance at all.

Examples

Discrete (every subset), indiscrete ($\{\varnothing,X\}$), finite complement ($U$ open iff $X\setminus U$ finite, or $U=\varnothing$).

§13 · Basis for a topology
§13
$x\in B_1\cap B_2\ \Rightarrow\ \exists B_3:\ x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$
Basis — and the topology it generates
▸
Symbols
  • $\mathcal B$the basis: a collection of subsets of $X$
  • $B_1,B_2,B_3$basis elements
Definition

$\mathcal B$ is a basis if every $x$ lies in some $B\in\mathcal B$, and the intersection condition above holds. The topology generated: $U$ is open iff for each $x\in U$ there is $B\in\mathcal B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$.

Lemma 13.1

The open sets are exactly the unions of basis elements.

Lemma 13.2

Conversely, if $\mathcal C$ is a collection of open sets such that every open $U$ and $x\in U$ have some $C\in\mathcal C$ with $x\in C\subseteq U$, then $\mathcal C$ is a basis for the topology.

Lemma 13.3
$\forall x\in B\in\mathcal B\ \ \exists B'\in\mathcal B':\ x\in B'\subseteq B$
Comparing topologies through bases
▸
Statement

$\mathcal T'$ (from $\mathcal B'$) is finer than $\mathcal T$ (from $\mathcal B$) if and only if the condition above holds.

Why

If $U$ is $\mathcal T$-open and $x\in U$, there is $B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$, then $B'$ with $x\in B'\subseteq B\subseteq U$. So $U$ is $\mathcal T'$-open.

Lemma 13.4

$\mathbb R_\ell$ and $\mathbb R_K$ are each strictly finer than $\mathbb R$, and not comparable with each other.

§15–16 · Products and subspaces
§15
$\mathcal B=\{U\times V:\ U\in\mathcal T_X,\ V\in\mathcal T_Y\}$
Product topology on X × Y
▸
Statement

The product topology is generated by open boxes. Theorem 15.1: products $B\times C$ of basis elements also form a basis.

Watch out

Not every open set is a box: an open disc in $\mathbb R^2$ is a union of boxes.

§16
$\mathcal T_Y=\{\,Y\cap U : U\in\mathcal T\,\}$
Subspace topology
▸
Statement

Open in $Y$ means: what an open set of $X$ cuts out of $Y$. Lemma 16.1: $\{B\cap Y\}$ is a basis for it.

Lemma 16.2

If $Y$ is open in $X$, sets open in $Y$ are open in $X$. Otherwise not: $[0,\tfrac12)$ is open in $[0,1]$ but not in $\mathbb R$.

Theorem 16.3

The product of subspaces carries the subspace topology of the product.

Closed sets, closures and limit points describe what a set "reaches toward". Hausdorff is the first separation property: distinct points can be pulled apart.

method
Show a space is Hausdorff
“Show X is Hausdorff”, often for a subspace, product or quotient
▸
Recognize it

You must separate every pair of distinct points.

Steps
  1. Take $x\neq y$. If points come in kinds, split into cases.

    Why: different kinds of pairs may need different constructions (in a quotient: two ordinary points, or an ordinary point and a glued one).

  2. In each case write down two open sets, one around each point.

    In metric spaces: balls of radius $d(x,y)/2$, made smaller if they must also avoid something.

  3. Prove they are disjoint: suppose $z$ is in both and reach a contradiction.

    Typical: $d(x,y)\le d(x,z)+d(z,y)\lt 2r\le d(x,y)$.

Uses

Hausdorff · Quotients.

Common mistakes
  • Separating only one pair, or only pairs of one kind.
Go deeper

To show a space is not Hausdorff, find two points such that every neighbourhood of one meets every neighbourhood of the other.

§17 · Closed sets and limit points
§17
$A\text{ closed}\iff X\setminus A\in\mathcal T$
Closed sets
▸
Theorem 17.1

$\varnothing$ and $X$ are closed; arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets are closed.

Theorems 17.2–17.4

In a subspace $Y$: $A$ is closed in $Y$ iff $A=C\cap Y$ for a closed $C$ in $X$; if $Y$ is closed in $X$, closed in $Y$ implies closed in $X$; the closure of $A$ in $Y$ is $\overline A\cap Y$.

Thm 17.5
$x\in\overline A\iff\text{every open }U\ni x\text{ meets }A$
The closure test
▸
Statement

Also with "every basis element containing $x$". This is the working definition of closure.

Why

If some open $U\ni x$ misses $A$, then $X\setminus U$ is a closed set containing $A$ but not $x$, so $x\notin\overline A$. Conversely each closed $C\supseteq A$ with $x\notin C$ gives the open $X\setminus C\ni x$ missing $A$.

Thm 17.6
$\overline A=A\cup A'$
Closure = the set plus its limit points
▸
Corollary 17.7

$A$ is closed iff it contains all its limit points.

Example

$A=(0,1)$: $A'=[0,1]$, so $\overline A=[0,1]$. The missing limit points $0,1$ are why crushing $(0,1)$ breaks Hausdorff (§22).

§17
$x\neq y\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,U\ni x,\ V\ni y:\ U\cap V=\varnothing$
Hausdorff spaces
▸
Theorem 17.8

Every finite set in a Hausdorff space is closed.

Theorem 17.10

In a Hausdorff space a sequence converges to at most one point.

Theorem 17.11

Order topologies are Hausdorff; products and subspaces of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff.

A continuous map pulls open sets back to open sets. Metric spaces are where intuition comes from: open means "a whole ball fits".

Show a function is continuous
“Show f is continuous”, “show f is a homeomorphism”
▸
Recognize it

A map between spaces given by bases or metrics.

Steps
  1. Take a basis element $B$ of $Y$ and show $f^{-1}(B)$ is open.

    Why: every open set is a union of basis elements, and preimages preserve unions.

  2. In metric spaces, for $x$ and $\varepsilon>0$ give $\delta>0$ with $d(x,y)\lt \delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(y))\lt \varepsilon$.

    Why: Theorem 21.1, the same condition in the language of balls.

  3. Or build $f$ from continuous pieces: compositions, restrictions, the pasting lemma, maps into products.

    Why: Theorems 18.2–18.4.

Uses

Continuity · Pasting.

Common mistakes
  • Checking images instead of preimages.
  • For a homeomorphism, forgetting the inverse.

Out of a quotient: $g:X^*\to Z$ is continuous iff $g\circ p$ is (Thm 22.2).

§18 · Continuous functions
§18
$V\in\mathcal T_Y\ \Rightarrow\ f^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal T_X$
Continuous function
▸
Symbols
  • $f:X\to Y$the map
  • $f^{-1}(V)$preimage: all $x$ with $f(x)\in V$
Example

Constant maps, the identity, inclusions of subspaces, projections from products.

Thm 18.1
$f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$
Equivalent forms of continuity
▸
Statement

For $f:X\to Y$ these are equivalent: (1) $f$ is continuous; (2) $f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$ for all $A$; (3) preimages of closed sets are closed; (4) for each $x$ and each neighbourhood $V$ of $f(x)$ there is a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ with $f(U)\subseteq V$.

Thm 18.3
$X=A\cup B,\ A,B\text{ closed}$
Pasting lemma
▸
Statement

If $f:A\to Y$ and $g:B\to Y$ are continuous and agree on $A\cap B$, then the combined map $h:X\to Y$ is continuous.

Why

For closed $C$: $h^{-1}(C)=f^{-1}(C)\cup g^{-1}(C)$, a union of two sets closed in $A$ and $B$, hence closed in $X$.

§18
$f,\ f^{-1}\text{ continuous}$
Homeomorphism
▸
Definition

A bijection $f:X\to Y$ with $f$ and $f^{-1}$ both continuous. Homeomorphic spaces are the same space with the points renamed.

Watch out

$[0,1)\to S^1$, $t\mapsto(\cos2\pi t,\sin2\pi t)$ is a continuous bijection, but its inverse tears the circle open, so it is not a homeomorphism.

§20–21 · Metric spaces
§20
$d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$
Metric and the metric topology
▸
Definition

$d(x,y)\ge0$ with equality iff $x=y$; $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$; the triangle inequality above. Basis: balls $B(x,\varepsilon)=\{y:d(x,y)\lt \varepsilon\}$.

Why balls are open

If $y\in B(x,\varepsilon)$, set $\delta=\varepsilon-d(x,y)$. Then $B(y,\delta)\subseteq B(x,\varepsilon)$ by the triangle inequality.

Hausdorff

For $x\neq y$ the balls of radius $d(x,y)/2$ are disjoint, so every metric space is Hausdorff.

Thm 21.1
$d(x,y)\lt \delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(y))\lt \varepsilon$
ε–δ continuity
▸
Statement

Between metric spaces, $f$ is continuous iff for every $x$ and $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with the property above.

A quotient glues points together. The topology is the most generous one that keeps the gluing map continuous, and the way to build its open sets is through saturated sets.

method
Work with a quotient
“Show X/∼ is Hausdorff”, “show the quotient is homeomorphic to …”
▸
Recognize it

A space built by gluing: an equivalence relation, a partition, or a map $p$.

Steps
  1. Describe the classes: the points of the quotient.

    Why: you need to know what you are separating or mapping. For $\sim_A$: the set $A$, and each $\{x\}$ with $x\notin A$.

  2. To show $V\subseteq X^*$ is open, check that $p^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$.

    Why: the definition of the quotient topology.

  3. To produce open sets in $X^*$, take open sets of $X$ that are saturated and push them down.

    Why: for saturated $C$, $p^{-1}(p(C))=C$, which is open.

    For a crushed set $A$: use open sets that miss $A$ or contain all of it.

  4. To map out of $X^*$, define a continuous map on $X$ that is constant on each class.

    Why: Theorem 22.2.

  • Pushing down an open set that contains part of a class.
  • Assuming quotients of nice spaces stay nice.

§22 · The quotient topology
§22
$U\subseteq X^*\text{ open}\iff p^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X$
Quotient topology and quotient map
▸
Symbols
  • $X^*$the set of pieces (classes)
  • $p:X\to X^*$sends each point to its piece
  • $p^{-1}(U)$everything lying over $U$
Quotient map

More generally a surjection $p:X\to Y$ is a quotient map if $U\subseteq Y$ is open exactly when $p^{-1}(U)$ is open.

Why A must be closed (HW7)

Crush $A=(0,1)\subset\mathbb R$. Every open set around $\{0\}$ pulls back to an open set containing $0$, which contains some $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$ and so points of $A$; so its image contains $[A]$. The two points cannot be separated.

§22
$C=p^{-1}(p(C))$
Saturated sets
▸
Statement

$C$ is saturated if it is a union of whole classes. Then $p^{-1}(p(C))=C$; if $C$ is also open, $p(C)$ is open in $X^*$.

For a crushed set

Any set containing all of $A$ or none of it.

Maps out of a quotient
▸
Statement

If $p:X\to X^*$ is a quotient map and $h:X\to Z$ is continuous and constant on each class, then $h$ induces $g:X^*\to Z$ with $g\circ p=h$, and $g$ is continuous.

Use

To show $[0,1]/\{0\sim1\}\cong S^1$: $h(t)=(\cos2\pi t,\sin2\pi t)$ is constant on $\{0,1\}$, so it induces a continuous bijection onto $S^1$; compactness then gives a homeomorphism (Ch. 3).

Spaces to test claims on. When something looks true, try it on these first; most counterexamples in the course are one of them.
space
$\mathbb R$
Standard line
▸
What it is

Basis: open intervals.

Properties

Metric, Hausdorff, connected.

space
$\mathbb R_\ell$
Lower limit line
▸
What it is

Basis: $[a,b)$.

Properties

Strictly finer than $\mathbb R$; Hausdorff; $[a,b)$ is open and closed.

space
$\mathbb R_K$
K-topology
▸
What it is

Basis: $(a,b)$ and $(a,b)\setminus K$, $K=\{1/n\}$.

Properties

Finer than $\mathbb R$; not comparable with $\mathbb R_\ell$; Hausdorff.

space
$\mathcal T_f$
Finite complement topology
▸
What it is

Open: complements of finite sets, and the empty set.

Properties

Points closed (T₁) but not Hausdorff on an infinite set: any two nonempty open sets meet.

space
$\mathcal P(X)$
Discrete
▸
What it is

Every subset is open.

Properties

Every map out of it is continuous.

space
$\{\varnothing,X\}$
Indiscrete
▸
What it is

Only the empty set and $X$.

Properties

Every map into it is continuous; not Hausdorff.

space
$\{0,1\}$
Sierpiński space
▸
What it is

Open: $\varnothing,\{1\},\{0,1\}$.

Properties

Not T₁: the point 0 is not closed.

space
$\mathbb R/(0,1)$
Crushing a non-closed set
▸
What it is

Glue $(0,1)$ to a point.

Properties

Not Hausdorff: $\{0\}$ and $[(0,1)]$ cannot be separated.

space
$[0,1]/\{0\sim1\}$
Circle from an interval
▸
What it is

Glue the two ends.

Properties

Homeomorphic to $S^1$.

space
$\mathbb R\sqcup\mathbb R/\!\sim$
Line with two origins
▸
What it is

Two copies of ℝ glued at every point except 0.

Properties

Looks like ℝ near every point, but the two origins cannot be separated.
